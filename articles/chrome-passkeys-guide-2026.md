## Understanding Passkeys and Why They Matter in 2026

Passkeys fundamentally change how you authenticate yourself online. Instead of relying on passwords that can be forgotten, stolen, or compromised, passkeys use cryptographic key pairs that are unique to each website and stored securely on your devices. When you create a passkey for a website, your device generates a public key that gets shared with the website and a private key that stays safely stored on your device. When you log in later, the website sends a challenge that your device can only answer with the corresponding private key, which is protected by your device's biometric authentication or screen lock.

The security benefits of passkeys are substantial and cannot be overstated. Traditional passwords are vulnerable to numerous attack vectors, including phishing websites that trick you into entering your credentials, data breaches that expose password databases, and keyloggers that record everything you type. Passkeys eliminate these vulnerabilities because the private key never leaves your device and cannot be replicated or transferred. Even if a website's entire database is compromised, attackers cannot use the public key to impersonate you since they would also need access to your physical device.

Phishing protection is perhaps the most compelling advantage of passkeys. Each passkey is cryptographically bound to a specific website domain, meaning it simply will not work on fake versions of the site. If you receive an email directing you to a malicious website that looks like your bank, your passkey will not authenticate because the cryptographic binding ensures the key only works on the legitimate domain. This automatic protection against phishing attacks works in the background without requiring any special action from you.

The adoption of passkeys has accelerated dramatically throughout 2025 and into 2026. Major technology companies including Google, Apple, and Microsoft have all committed to supporting passkeys, and countless websites have implemented the technology. Banking institutions, healthcare portals, government services, and social media platforms now commonly offer passkey authentication. This widespread adoption means you can use passkeys for most of your important online accounts, making the transition away from passwords increasingly practical.

## Creating Passkeys in Chrome

Creating your first passkey in Chrome is straightforward, though the exact process varies slightly depending on the website. The first step is ensuring your Chrome browser is up to date, as Google continuously improves passkey support with new features and security enhancements. To check for updates, click the three dots in the upper right corner of Chrome, select Help, and choose About Google Chrome. If an update is available, install it and restart your browser.

Before creating passkeys, verify that your device supports the necessary authentication methods. Passkeys work with biometric authentication like fingerprint readers or facial recognition, as well as device screen locks including PINs, patterns, and passwords. Most modern computers, smartphones, and tablets support at least one of these methods. On Windows computers, you can use Windows Hello with a PIN, fingerprint, or facial recognition. Mac users can leverage Touch ID on compatible laptops or their Apple Watch for authentication. Android devices typically support fingerprint or face unlock, while iPhones and iPads use Face ID or Touch ID.

The actual process of creating a passkey begins with logging into your target website using your existing password. Once logged in, navigate to your account settings or security settings where you manage login options. Look for options labeled Create Passkey, Add Passkey, Enable Passwordless Login, or similar wording. Different websites use different terminology, but the option will always relate to passkeys or passwordless authentication.

When you initiate passkey creation, Chrome will display a dialog asking which device should store the passkey. If you are creating the passkey on the computer you are currently using, select that device from the list. If you prefer to use your smartphone instead, ensure it is connected to the same Google account and appears in the available devices list. The ability to choose which device stores your passkey provides flexibility for how you want to authenticate in the future.

After selecting your device, you must verify your identity to complete the passkey creation. This verification uses your device's existing authentication method, whether that is fingerprint scanning, face recognition, or entering your screen lock PIN. This step ensures that only you can create passkeys for your accounts. Once verified, Chrome generates the cryptographic key pair and stores the private key securely. The website receives the public key, which it stores associated with your account. You should receive confirmation from both Chrome and the website that the passkey was created successfully.

For the best experience, create passkeys on multiple devices that you regularly use. Chrome can synchronize passkeys across devices through your Google account, but having the passkey stored locally on each device provides the most seamless experience. When creating a passkey, you will be prompted to choose which devices should have access, and you can select multiple devices if desired.

## Using Passkeys to Log In to Websites

Logging in with a passkey is noticeably faster and easier than typing a password. When you return to a website where you have set up a passkey, you will see an option to use your passkey instead of entering a password. This option typically appears as a prominent button or as the default authentication method. Clicking this option triggers the authentication process.

Chrome will prompt you to confirm that you want to use your passkey for authentication. After confirmation, your device will request biometric verification or ask for your screen lock PIN. Complete this verification, and you are logged in within seconds. The entire process typically takes just two to three seconds, compared to the much longer time required to locate, type, and potentially correct a password.

The seamless experience extends across different scenarios. On computers with fingerprint readers, you can simply tap the sensor. On smartphones and tablets, Face ID or Touch ID provides instant authentication. If your device does not have biometric capabilities, entering your screen lock PIN is quick and familiar. This consistency across different devices and websites makes passkey authentication not only more secure but also more convenient than any alternative.

It is important to understand that each passkey is bound to a specific website domain. A passkey created for yourbank.com will only work on yourbank.com and not on any other website, even if that website has a similar name or appearance. This domain binding is a fundamental security feature that protects you from phishing attacks. When you visit the legitimate website, your passkey automatically works. When you accidentally visit a fake website, the passkey simply does not function, alerting you to the potential threat.

Some websites offer additional authentication options alongside passkeys, such as sending a code to your phone or using an authenticator app. While these alternatives can be useful in certain situations, passkeys provide the best combination of security and convenience. When given the choice, always prefer passkey authentication for the strongest protection of your account.

## Syncing Passkeys Across All Your Devices

One of the most powerful features of passkeys in Chrome is the ability to synchronize them across all your devices through your Google account. This synchronization means that when you create a passkey on one device, it becomes available on all other devices where you are signed in with the same Google account. This convenience eliminates the need to create separate passkeys for each device and ensures you always have access to your accounts.

To enable passkey synchronization, ensure you are signed into Chrome with your Google account on all devices where you want to use passkeys. This sign-in is separate from being logged into individual websites; it is the Chrome browser itself that needs your Google account. Check your sync settings by clicking your profile icon in Chrome and verifying that sync is enabled. Within sync settings, ensure that passwords and passkeys are set to sync.

When you create a passkey while signed into your Google account, you will be asked which devices should have access. You can choose to store the passkey on your current device only, or you can select other devices connected to your account. Making an informed choice here helps ensure your passkeys are available where you need them.

Passkeys sync seamlessly between Chrome on Windows, macOS, Linux, Chrome OS, and Android. iOS devices can also receive synced passkeys through Chrome, though the native Safari browser handles iPhone and iPad passkeys differently. If you use Chrome on multiple platforms, your passkeys will be available across all of them once sync is properly configured.

Managing your synced passkeys is done through Google Password Manager, which also handles traditional passwords. Access this manager by clicking the three dots in Chrome, selecting Passwords and Autofill, and then choosing Google Password Manager. Here you can view all your saved passwords and passkeys, delete passkeys for websites you no longer use, and see which devices have access to each passkey.

When you first use a synced passkey on a new device, you may need to verify your identity again to confirm your ownership of the account. This additional verification is a security measure that prevents unauthorized access even if someone gains access to your synced passkey data. After the initial verification, using the passkey on that device becomes as simple as using it on any other.

There are some limitations to be aware of regarding passkey synchronization. Passkeys created in other browsers like Safari or Firefox do not automatically appear in Chrome, as each browser maintains its own secure credential storage. Additionally, some websites may limit which devices can use a passkey for authentication, though this is relatively uncommon. For the most consistent experience, create passkeys directly in Chrome on each device where you want to use them.

## Replacing Passwords with Passkeys

Making the transition from passwords to passkeys is a process that happens gradually as more websites support the technology. While not every website has implemented passkey support yet, enough major services have adopted passkeys that you can begin replacing passwords for most of your important accounts. This section provides strategies for systematically transitioning to a password-free future.

Start by identifying your most critical accounts and prioritizing them for passkey conversion. Your email accounts should be at the top of this list because they serve as recovery options for many other services. Once you secure your email with a passkey, you significantly improve your overall security posture. Banking and financial accounts are also high priorities, as these represent the most valuable targets for attackers. Social media accounts, shopping sites, and productivity tools follow in priority.

The process for converting each account follows a similar pattern. Log into the website using your existing password, navigate to security settings, and look for the passkey creation option. Create the passkey, verify your identity, and then test that the passkey works by logging out and logging back in. Once you confirm the passkey works, you can choose whether to keep the old password as a backup or remove it entirely for maximum security.

For accounts that do not yet support passkeys, continue using strong, unique passwords managed through Chrome's built-in password manager. Chrome can generate secure random passwords for new accounts and store them encrypted on your device. The combination of passkeys for supported sites and Chrome's password manager for others provides excellent security coverage for all your online activities.

As you convert accounts to passkeys, consider enabling two-factor authentication where available even for passkey-protected accounts. While passkeys themselves provide strong protection, adding an additional verification layer further enhances security. Many websites now offer passkey plus second factor combinations that provide exceptional protection against unauthorized access.

Managing the transition period requires attention to backup options. Some users prefer to keep their passwords as emergency backups while they test passkey functionality. This approach provides a safety net during the transition but gradually becomes unnecessary as you gain confidence in passkey reliability. Eventually, you can remove saved passwords for accounts that now use passkeys, reducing your attack surface.

Regularly reviewing your passkey coverage helps ensure you are maximizing the security benefits. Set a goal to convert a certain number of accounts each week until you have covered all your important services. This systematic approach makes the transition manageable and ensures you do not overlook any significant accounts.

## Troubleshooting Passkey Issues

Even though passkeys are designed to work seamlessly, you may occasionally encounter issues. Understanding common problems and their solutions helps you maintain a smooth authentication experience. Most issues can be resolved quickly without requiring extensive technical knowledge.

One common issue occurs when passkeys do not appear to work after creating them. If you visit a website where you created a passkey but do not see the passkey login option, first verify you are using the same browser and device where you created the passkey. Check that you are signed into the same Google account that was active when you created the passkey. If the website offers multiple authentication methods, look carefully for the passkey option, as it may be labeled differently or positioned less prominently than the password field.

Authentication failures can occur if your device's biometric enrollment has changed. If you added a new fingerprint or recalibrated face recognition, you may need to verify that Chrome recognizes your current biometric data. Restarting your device often resolves authentication issues by ensuring all biometric services are running properly. On mobile devices, make sure Chrome has permission to use biometrics in your device settings.

Cross-device passkey issues are particularly common. If you created a passkey on your phone but want to use it on your computer, ensure both devices are signed into the same Google account and that sync is enabled. Sometimes waiting a few minutes for synchronization to complete resolves the issue. If the passkey still does not appear, try creating a new passkey directly on the computer where you want to use it.

Browser-specific issues can arise from cached data or conflicting extensions. Try clearing your browser cache and cookies for the affected website, or test the passkey in incognito mode to determine if the issue is related to stored data. If you use privacy or security extensions that modify how websites function, temporarily disable them to see if they are interfering with passkey authentication.

When websites report technical difficulties with passkeys, the issue is often on their end rather than with Chrome. Check if the website has published any notices about passkey problems or scheduled maintenance. You can also try using a different authentication method temporarily while the website resolves technical issues. As passkey technology matures, these server-side problems become increasingly rare.

Device compatibility remains a consideration, particularly with older hardware. If your computer does not have a fingerprint reader or camera for biometric authentication, you can still use passkeys with your screen lock PIN or password. Ensure your device's security features are properly configured and functioning before attempting to use passkeys.
